01 — The Basics

What phishing actually is

Phishing is a social engineering attack: instead of hacking a system, the attacker hacks a person's trust, urgency, or curiosity to get them to hand over credentials, money, or access.

Mass

Email phishing

Generic, high-volume messages impersonating a known brand or service, sent to thousands of people hoping a small percentage bite.

Targeted

Spear phishing

Personalized messages built using research on you or your role — your manager's name, a real project, a real vendor — to feel legitimate.

Executive

Whaling

Spear phishing aimed at executives or finance staff, usually impersonating a CEO or CFO to authorize a wire transfer or data release.

Voice

Vishing

Phone calls impersonating IT support, a bank, or a delivery service to extract credentials or one-time passcodes verbally.

SMS

Smishing

Text messages with urgent delivery, billing, or security alerts containing a shortened or spoofed link.

Internal

Business email compromise

An attacker impersonates or hijacks a real colleague's account to redirect a legitimate invoice or payment.

Manufactured urgency. A tight deadline and alarming tone are designed to make you act before you think — a core social engineering lever.

Generic greeting. A real service you have an account with usually addresses you by name, not a placeholder.

We have detect unusual activity on you're account and needs you to confirm you're identity immediately to avoid permanent suspension.

Grammar and spelling errors. "detect" instead of "detected," "you're" instead of "your" — inconsistent with professional communication from a real company.

Mismatched link destination. The button text says "verify your account," but hovering would reveal it actually points to a completely unrelated, unofficial domain — never trust link text alone.

Unexpected executable-style attachment. A .zip claiming to be a statement is a common way to smuggle malware — legitimate statements are almost never sent as a compressed file you didn't request.
03 — Spotting Fake Websites

Real domain or lookalike?

Attackers rely on you skimming a URL rather than reading it. For each pair, click the one you believe is the legitimate site.

https://www.paypa1.com/login
https://www.paypal.com/login
The first domain replaces the letter "l" with the number "1" — a classic typosquat that's nearly invisible in most fonts at a glance.
https://accounts.google.com/signin
https://accounts.google.com.verify-secure.net
The domain that matters is the part right before the first single slash. "accounts.google.com.verify‑secure.net" is actually a subdomain of verify-secure.net — "google.com" there is just text, not the real domain.
http://bankofamerica-secure-login.com
https://www.bankofamerica.com
Two signals here: an unrelated hyphenated domain tacked onto a brand name, and plain http:// instead of https:// — no padlock, no encryption.
04 — The Human Angle

Tactics attackers lean on

The technology in a phishing email is simple. What makes it work is psychology. These are the levers attackers pull most often.

Authority

Impersonating a boss, IT admin, or executive so the request feels non-negotiable.

A well-documented pattern: business email compromise scams have impersonated company executives to direct finance staff to wire large sums to attacker-controlled accounts — costing organizations worldwide billions of dollars in aggregate over the past decade.

Urgency

A ticking clock ("24 hours," "immediate action required") short-circuits careful thinking.

Countdown timers, account suspension threats, and "final notice" language are almost always a manufactured pressure tactic.

Fear

Threatening consequences — legal action, account loss, exposure — to provoke a panicked click.

Fake "your account has been compromised" alerts are one of the most common fear-based lures in circulation.

Curiosity

A vague, intriguing subject line ("Re: your photo," "Invoice attached") that invites a click just to find out what it is.

Attackers often reuse real subject-line conventions from internal tools so the message blends in with everyday traffic.

Trust exploitation

Hijacking or spoofing a real, known contact's identity so the request appears to come from someone you already trust.

In a widely reported 2020 incident, attackers used targeted phone-based social engineering against employees at a major social platform to gain access to internal admin tools.

Pretexting

Building a believable backstory — a fake IT ticket, a fake delivery issue — before ever making the ask.

Vishing calls often open with a plausible, low-stakes story before pivoting to a request for a password or one-time code.
05 — Defense

What to actually do

A short list you can genuinely keep in your head.

→
Check the sender's real address, not the display name.

Display names can say anything. Tap or hover to see the actual email address behind it.

→
Hover before you click.

On desktop, hovering over a link shows the real destination in your browser or email client's status bar.

→
Slow down when you feel urgency.

Urgency is a manufactured signal, not a reason to skip verification. A real deadline can survive a two-minute check.

→
Verify through a second channel.

If an email or call asks for money, credentials, or access, confirm it by calling a known number or messaging the person directly — not by replying to the message itself.

→
Never enter credentials from an email link.

Navigate to the site yourself by typing the address or using a saved bookmark instead.

→
Treat unexpected attachments as hostile by default.

Especially .zip, .exe, .js, or macro-enabled Office files you didn't ask for.

→
Report it — don't just delete it.

Reporting helps your security team block the sender for everyone else before more people are targeted.
